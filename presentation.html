Standardization

Understanding Confounding and Population Structure

Erik Westlund

2025-10-16

Motivating Example: Delayed Seeking of Necessary Healthcare

  • Motivation: People wait to get help until a health issue becomes serious or avoidable harm has already occurred
    • Skipping routine visits or screenings until symptoms worsen
    • Delaying care due to cost, time, or caregiving demands
    • Arriving at the health system only during a crisis
  • Importance: Reveals inequities in access, prevention, and confidence in the health system
  • Question For Us: Who faces greater risk (e.g., men or women?) and how can we compare that risk fairly?

The Challenge

Crude rates can mislead us about who is at risk and why

Meeting Themes

Refresher:

  • Sex-disaggregated data tells us who (counts)
  • Gendered analysis asks why — how roles, norms, and power shape health and behavior
  • Those processes interact with age and the gender equality context


This lecture’s focus: How causal structure and population composition determine what crude rates actually mean

Agenda

  1. The core idea: Statistical expectation and population structure
  2. Example 1: Age → Outcome (composition matters)
  3. Example 2: Age + Sex → Outcome (Simpson’s paradox)
  4. Example 3: Age + Sex + Gender Opportunity → Outcome (limits of manual standardization)
  5. Summary: Getting the expected value right

The Core Problem

  • Every population has its own mix of people — by age, gender, opportunity, and access.
  • That mix shapes what we see in the data: who gets sick, who shows up, and when.
  • When we compare groups without accounting for those differences, we risk blaming behavior or biology for what is really structure.

Statistical Expectation

  • The expected rate is what we’d see if we looked across everyone in a group, given their mix of ages and circumstances.
  • If two groups have different mixes, their expected rates aren’t directly comparable.
  • We get the wrong picture because we’re averaging over different kinds of people.

How Standardization Fixes It

  • Step 1: Agree on a common mix — the same age, gender, or opportunity structure for everyone.
  • Step 2: Recalculate each group’s rate as if they had that same mix.
  • Result: Differences now reflect real risk, not who happens to be older, poorer, or more constrained.


In short: Standardization answers, “What would these rates look like if all groups lived under the same conditions?”

Example 1: Age → Outcome

The Simplest Case: Age Only

Research question: What’s the delayed healthcare seeking rate in our population?

  • We have simulated registry data on preventable health crises
  • E.g., diabetic emergencies, hypertensive crises, or late-stage cancers
  • We define the risk by age: Young: 5%, Middle: 15%, Older: 40%
  • Question: What crude rate do we expect?


Key insight: Depends entirely on population age structure!

Causal Structure: Age → Outcome

No confounding here. Just one cause: Age

But crude rate still depends on age.

Simulated Dataset 1: Age Distribution

True Age-Specific Rates (Known)

Computing the Expected Value

Using the expectation formula:

\[ E[Y] = \sum_{\text{age}} E[Y \mid \text{Age}] \times P(\text{Age}) \]

# Our age-specific rates (known truth)
rates <- c(Young = 0.05, Middle = 0.15, Older = 0.40)

# Our population weights
weights <- c(Young = 0.30, Middle = 0.35, Older = 0.35)

# Expected value
expected_rate <- sum(rates * weights)
expected_rate
[1] 0.2075

Expected crude rate: 20.7%

This is what we should see in the data.

Observed vs Expected

Key insight: Crude rate reflects population age structure. Different population → different crude rate.

What If We Had a Different Population?

  • Imagine Population A: Mostly young people → low crude rate
  • Imagine Population B: Mostly older people → high crude rate
  • Same age-specific rates (5%, 15%, 40%)
  • Different crude rates because \(\text{Age}\) differs


This is why standardization matters!

To compare Population A vs B fairly, we need to apply their age-specific rates to a standard population and use the same age weights for both

Example 2: Age + Sex

Adding Gender: Simpson’s Paradox

Research question: Do men or women have higher late-presentation rates?

  • Now we have Sex and Age in our data
  • Known truth (from simulation):
    • Men have higher risk within every age group (by a few percentage points)
    • Women live longer → more older women
  • What will crude rates show?

Causal Structure: Age + Sex → Outcome

This is confounding!

Sex affects Age (women live longer), and Age affects Outcome.

Crude comparison of men vs women is biased by age composition.

Simulated Dataset 2: Age Structure by Sex

Key: P(Age | Man) ≠ P(Age | Woman), but marginal P(Age) same as Dataset 1

Crude Rates by Sex

Observation: Women have higher crude rate

But is this the truth?

This is Simpson’s Paradox.

Visualizing Simpson’s Paradox

What Went Wrong?

Remember the expectation formula:

\[ E[Y \mid \text{Sex}] = \sum_{\text{age}} E[Y \mid \text{Sex}, \text{Age}] \times P(\text{Age} \mid \text{Sex}) \]

  • \(P(\text{Age} \mid \text{Man})\) is different from \(P(\text{Age} \mid \text{Woman})\)
  • Women have more weight on older ages (where everyone has high rates)
  • Men have more weight on younger ages (where everyone has low rates)
  • Men have higher risk within every age group
  • Different age weights flip the crude pattern despite consistent within-age differences

Indirect Standardization

Goal: What would the expected number of events be if each group had standard rates?

# Step 1: Use overall population rates as standard
standard_rates <- sim2_data %>%
  group_by(age_group) %>%
  summarise(std_rate = mean(late_presentation))

# Step 2: Calculate expected events for each sex using standard rates
indirect_std <- sim2_data %>%
  group_by(sex, age_group) %>%
  summarise(n = n(), .groups = "drop") %>%
  left_join(standard_rates, by = "age_group") %>%
  group_by(sex) %>%
  summarise(
    observed = sum(n * age_sex_rates$rate[match(paste(sex, age_group),
                                                  paste(age_sex_rates$sex, age_sex_rates$age_group))]),
    expected = sum(n * std_rate),
    SMR = observed / expected
  )

indirect_std
# A tibble: 2 × 4
  sex   observed expected   SMR
  <fct>    <dbl>    <dbl> <dbl>
1 Man       2220    2081. 1.07 
2 Woman     2217    2356. 0.941

SMR: Standardized Morbidity Ratio

Interpretation: Men have 7% higher risk than expected from standard rates

Direct Standardization: The Fix

Goal: What would rates be if men and women had the same age distribution?

# Step 1: Use marginal P(Age) as standard weights
std_weights <- sim2_data %>%
  count(age_group) %>%
  mutate(weight = n / sum(n))

# Step 2: Apply to sex-age specific rates
direct_std <- age_sex_rates %>%
  left_join(std_weights, by = "age_group") %>%
  group_by(sex) %>%
  summarise(standardized_rate = sum(rate * weight))

direct_std
# A tibble: 2 × 2
  sex   standardized_rate
  <fct>             <dbl>
1 Man               0.237
2 Woman             0.209

Crude vs Standardized Rates

Pattern reverses! Standardization reveals the truth: men have higher risk.

Key Insight: Confounding

  • Crude rates get it wrong because Sex → Age and Age → Outcome
  • Age is a confounder of the Sex-Outcome relationship
  • Direct standardization fixes \(P(\text{Age})\) to break the confounding
  • Result: Correct comparison of sex differences


Next: What happens when we have multiple confounders?

Example 3: Age + Sex + GOC

Adding Gender Opportunity Context: Multiple Confounders

Research question: Same question, but now we also have GOC data

GOC = Gender Opportunity Context (gendered norms/power/access)

  • Known truth (from simulation):
    • Age effect: Young=5%, Middle=15%, Older=40%
    • Sex effect: Men higher within age groups (constant logit-scale effect)
    • GOC effect: Low/Mid higher than High (constant logit-scale effects)

Multiple Confounding Pathways

  • GOC → Age (low GOC = younger; more constraints/shorter healthy lifespan)
  • GOC → Outcome (low GOC = higher risk; barriers to access/norms)
  • Sex → Age (women live longer)
  • Age → Outcome
  • Sex → Outcome


The complexity: All three variables affect both each other AND the outcome

Causal Structure: Multiple Confounders

This is complex!

To compare groups fairly, we need to adjust for Age AND GOC.

Manual standardization becomes unmanageable.

Simulated Dataset 3: Age by Sex and GOC

Crude Rates: Confounded

  group_by(goc) %>%

# A tibble: 3 × 2
  goc   crude_rate
  <fct>      <dbl>
1 Low        0.246
2 Mid        0.259
3 High       0.249

All confounded! Low GOC appears to have lower risk (wrong!) because low GOC contexts are younger.

Why Manual Standardization Fails

To standardize by Age and GOC:

  • Need age-GOC-specific rates for each sex: 3 ages × 3 GOC = 9 strata per sex
  • With small cell counts, rates become unstable
  • Adding more confounders (ethnicity, region, comorbidity) → exponential growth
  • Becomes unmanageable quickly


Solution: Regression models do multivariable standardization automatically

Poisson Regression

glm_fit <- glm(late_presentation ~ sex + age_group + goc,
               family = poisson(link = "log"),
               data = sim3_data)
  • Fits a model: \(\log(E[Y \mid \text{Sex}, \text{Age}, \text{GOC}]) = \beta_0 + \beta_{\text{Sex}} + \beta_{\text{Age}} + \beta_{\text{GOC}}\)
  • Automatically adjusts for all confounders simultaneously
  • This is standardization! Model weights by the data’s covariate distribution

Adjusted Probabilities: Men vs Women

library(emmeans)

# Get marginal probabilities by sex (adjusted for age and GOC)
emmeans(glm_fit, ~ sex, type = "response")

What the Model Does

  • Model fits on log scale: \(\log(E[Y \mid \text{Sex}, \text{Age}, \text{GOC}]) = \beta_0 + \beta_{\text{Sex}} + \beta_{\text{Age}} + \beta_{\text{GOC}}\)
  • emmeans converts those to predicted probabilities we can interpret
  • Then calculates probability differences between groups
  • Equivalent to standardizing by Age and GOC simultaneously
  • Much more manageable than 9 strata of manual standardization

Summary

The Story

  1. Example 1 (Age only): Crude rate depends on population structure
  2. Example 2 (Age + Sex): Confounding creates Simpson’s Paradox; direct standardization fixes it
  3. Example 3 (Age + Sex + GOC): Multiple confounders: regression models generalize standardization

All Methods Compared

Getting the Expected Value Right Requires Understanding Causal Structure
Method Example 2 (Sex) Example 3 (GOC + Sex)
Crude Women appear worse Low GOC appears better (wrong!)
Direct Standardization Men worse (correct) Too many strata (unmanageable)
Poisson GLM Men worse (RR ≈ 1.06) Men worse, Low GOC worse (correct)

Key Takeaways

  1. Expectation = weighted average of subgroup rates
  2. Population structure determines which weights we use
  3. Confounding occurs when groups have different structures
  4. Standardization uses common weights to make fair comparisons
  5. Regression models = automated multivariable standardization

Connecting to Policy

If we only looked at crude rates:

  • Example 2: Crude data: women seem worse. Truth: men higher risk
  • Example 3: Crude data: low-opportunity contexts seem safer. Truth: risk highest where gender equality is lowest
  • Wrong interventions: Misallocated resources, ineffective programs
  • Root cause: Ignored causal structure and population composition

Gendered health analysis requires understanding confounding, causal structure, and standardization
